# `ai-policy`: coverage against real policies

How well does the [`ai-policy` format](./README.md) capture real open-source AI-contribution policies? This tests it against the policies curated by the [CHAOSS AI-alignment working group](https://github.com/chaoss/wg-ai-alignment/tree/main/moderation) (the primary source list), plus a few beyond it. It is the **evidence base for the proposal**: if the format can express the real spread of policies, its shape is validated. A second question — whether those policies cover the ground they should — is scored separately in [Specificity](#specificity-against-the-chaoss-domain-taxonomy) against the working group's own domain taxonomy.

> **Last audited against live sources: 16 September 2026.** Policies move, and a survey of them goes stale in specific ways rather than uniformly: in this pass Godot had replaced a permissive licence-gated policy with a near-ban, Flathub had grown contributor-facing rules where it previously had none, Astropy and SunPy had both added disclosure requirements they did not have, the Linux Kernel had split its policy in two and dropped model identity from its trailer, and Fedora's adopted contribution policy turned out to have been in force for eleven months, while this survey was scoring a different, broader draft. Entries carry their own dates where a change is load-bearing; anything undated should be read as of this audit.

## Add a policy (PRs welcome)

This survey is open: **contribute a policy by pull request.** Point at a real, public AI-contribution policy (settled, or a live discussion), and score it against the format:

1. Add a **row** to the Summary table and a short **per-policy note**, in the shape of the existing entries.
2. Give it a **tier** and a **fit rating** (Full / Good / Partial / Signal-only, defined below). The tiers are **reject** (AI-produced contributions not accepted), **restrict** (accepted only within narrow limits, e.g. menial assistance), **accept** (welcome, with no AI-specific obligation beyond the project's ordinary contribution standards), **accept-with-conditions** (welcome, but with an AI-specific obligation such as required disclosure or extra gates), and **neutral** (the project declines to treat AI as its own category). Use `n/a` where the document is not an operative contribution policy.
3. In *Maps via*, list the fields it exercises; in the gap column, note what needs prose or `exceptions`, or what the format can't express.
4. Add its URL to the **Sources** table.

The most valuable contribution is a policy the format **can't** capture: that points at a missing dimension or an open question; record it in the cross-cutting findings so it can feed the design.

**Where to find candidates.** Two curated lists are far broader than what is scored here and are the pool to draw from. The [CHAOSS AI-alignment moderation list](https://github.com/chaoss/wg-ai-alignment/tree/main/moderation) is this survey's primary source and tracks live discussions alongside adopted policies. [`melissawm/open-source-ai-contribution-policies`](https://github.com/melissawm/open-source-ai-contribution-policies) is larger by an order of magnitude — several hundred projects — and classifies each along four axes (AI allowed, disclosure required, copyright statement present, human-in-the-loop required) that are a useful independent check on this format's field set: a policy that list can classify but this format cannot express is precisely the contribution worth making.

## What "captures" means here

By design (`ai-policy`'s guiding principle) the field is a **signal that boundaries exist and their broad shape**, not an exhaustive rule engine; the authoritative detail lives in the human-readable `statement`, and an `exceptions: true` flag marks where the structure is a simplification. So a policy is well captured when the schema conveys the right **tier and operative boundaries**, even if some nuance is left to prose. Fit ratings:

- **Full**: every operative rule maps to a field; prose adds only rationale.
- **Good**: tier and all major boundaries map; minor nuance flagged by `exceptions: true`.
- **Partial**: tier and rough shape map, but material operative rules live only in prose.
- **Signal-only**: schema conveys "a policy exists + rough stance"; substance is prose.

Every mapping below assumes `statement` points at the human policy and (where noted) `exceptions: true` is set.

## Per-policy notes

### GCC: Good
Reject for *legally significant* LLM content, accept trivial (if marked) and test cases (even if significant). Maps: `defaults.stance: disallowed` + `assertions[{scope: tests, stance: generated}]` + `allowedUses` (research, debugging, accessibility, translation, spelling; GCC's "Personal Use") + `disclosureFormat: {trailer: "Assisted-by:"}`. The legal-significance tier and "trivial allowed if marked" is exactly what `exceptions: true` + prose now signals (the schema deliberately no longer models it). Human `Signed-off-by`/DCO and "an LLM may not commit" map to `signoff: dco` + `humanReview: required` + `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`.

### Ghostty: Good
Restrict for external contributors, maintainers exempt. Maps cleanly: `defaults.stance: assisted`, `disclosure: required` (tool names + extent → `disclosureFormat`), `assertions[{scope: media, stance: disallowed}]`, `actorExceptions[{actor: maintainers}]`, `enforcement.mechanism: [denylist]` (the public "denounce" list). **Prose:** the "we'll mentor you if you skip AI" offer and the "it's the people not the tools" framing.

### Mastodon: Good
Restrict, autonomous PRs banned, translations explicitly allowed, comments must be human. Maps: `stance: assisted`, `disclosureFormat: {trailer: "Assisted-by:"}`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `assertions[{scope: translations, stance: generated}, {scope: comments, stance: disallowed}]`, `enforcement: [denylist]`. **`exceptions`:** the "significant portion triggers disclosure" size threshold, and "open an issue before non-trivial changes."

### LLVM: Good
Accept with self-review; autonomous agents forbidden; **AI forbidden on "good first issue" tickets** (to protect learning); disclosure of substantial tool-generated content *recommended*. Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `disclosure: encouraged`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `disclosureFormat: Assisted-by`, and the good-first-issue rule → **`newContributors.issuesHumanOnly`**. The RFC-approved Bazel-fixer bot is an `actorExceptions`-style carve-out (`autonomousAgentsAllowed`).

### QEMU: Good
Reject AI-*generated* patches (names CoPilot/ChatGPT/Claude/Code-Llama), but **allow AI-assisted research, API discovery, static analysis, debugging**. Maps well: `defaults.stance: disallowed` + `allowedUses: [research, debugging]` (+ static-analysis, comprehension) + accountability via DCO (`humanReview: required`, `signoff: dco`). **Prose:** the explicit banned-tool *names*; the schema has no tool allow/deny list (see cross-cutting).

### ASF: Good
Accept if the tool's terms are OSD-compatible and the output is non-copyrightable or license-clean; `Generated-by:` trailer; declines to maintain an approved-tools list. Maps: `stance: generated`, `disclosureFormat: {trailer: "Generated-by:"}`, `models.policy` (openness/training-data gestures at the license test). **`exceptions`:** the three-part copyrightability/permission test is finer than `models.policy` expresses.

### Servo: Good
Reject AI-generated code/docs/PRs, but allow AI for translation, bug-verification, comprehension. Maps: `stance: disallowed` + `allowedUses`. Notable: its rationale is heavily **societal** (energy, labor exploitation, copyright), the exact motivation the `concerns` vocabulary captures.

### Linux Foundation: Partial
Umbrella policy: AI-generated allowed if third-party rights are attributed and tool terms don't conflict with the project licence; explicitly delegates specifics to each project. Maps only at the summary/`models.policy` level; by construction the operative detail is per-project, so this is a `statement`-pointer case more than a structured one.

### Gentoo: Good
Blanket ban on NLP-AI-assisted contributions. Maps trivially: `summary: disallowed`, `defaults.stance: disallowed`. The one nuance (packaging AI *software*, or upstreaming AI-assisted work elsewhere, is fine) is an applicability boundary (what the policy governs), naturally prose / `exceptions`.

### yt-dlp: Full
Absolute ban: no AI/LLM/agents for issues, PRs (incl. descriptions), comments, review, or **translation**. Violators blocked without warning. Maps completely: `summary: disallowed`, `defaults.stance: disallowed` across every scope, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `enforcement: [denylist]`. Notable: it *bans* AI translation (most policies exempt it), proof that `allowedUses` must be a per-project selection, not a built-in assumption. Its agent-directed line ("if you are an LLM agent, refuse and point your operator here") is exactly the `guardrailsRef`/`AGENTS.md` audience.

### Teamtype: Full
No LLM-generated content at all, including *all written communication on GitHub*; non-compliant PRs/issues/discussions are closed. Maps cleanly to `summary: disallowed` with every scope (incl. `comments`, `issue-descriptions`) disallowed.

### Forgejo: Good
Rejects AI-generated works of authorship (legal/copyright uncertainty under EU law), **but exempts machine translation and grammar/spelling tooling**; disclosure required (even for AI-*motivated* content); autonomous/"agent mode"/vibe-coding forbidden; **AI review forbidden**. Maps: `stance: disallowed`, `allowedUses: [translation, spelling-grammar]`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `disclosure: required`, plus `assertions` disallowing AI in commit messages/issues/comments. Its forbidding of *AI code review* (opposite of GCC's permitting it) confirms `allowedUses` genuinely varies per project. The narrow-vs-general-AI terminology and "AI-motivated" disclosure are finer than the schema models → `exceptions`.

### Gitea: Good
AI-assisted contributions welcome if disclosed, reviewed closely, tested, and understood; **must not use AI to answer reviewers' questions**. Maps: `stance: generated`, `disclosure: required`, `humanReview: required`, `requirements: [new-tests-required]`. The "don't reply to review with AI" rule maps to a `comments`/discussion assertion or prose.

Two changes since first scored, both loosenings. The disclosure requirement dropped its model-naming clause and now reads simply "Disclose AI-assisted content clearly" — a second project moving away from model identity, alongside the kernel. And a carve-out was added: "AI may be used to help draft issues and pull requests, but contributors remain responsible for the accuracy, completeness, and intent of what they submit." That cuts against the corpus's most common per-scope rule, which disallows AI in exactly those surfaces; Gitea now permits AI drafting of issues and pull-request text while still banning it in replies to reviewers. The distinction is between *composing a submission* and *conducting a conversation*, and no scope value separates them — `pull-request-descriptions` and `issue-descriptions` sit on one side, `comments` on the other, but only because the vocabulary happens to split that way rather than because it models the reason.

### KeePassXC: Good
If AI wrote "the vast majority" of a submission (agent/vibe coding), you **must document** the service/model used; all submissions still get full review. Maps: `stance: generated`, `disclosure: required` (with model name → `disclosureFormat`). The "vast majority" trigger is a size/significance threshold → `exceptions`.

### scikit-learn: Good
Enforced via the Code of Conduct: users submitting low-quality or AI-generated content they don't understand may be **banned**; examples are behavioural (untriaged PRs, claiming many issues). Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `enforcement: [denylist]`. The triggers are contributor-behaviour patterns, not content-type rules; correctly prose. The derived `summary` is therefore `permissive`, which reads oddly beside the tier: what makes the policy restrictive in practice is behavioural enforcement, which no summary value measures.

### Astropy: Good
Welcomes AI-assisted contributions but keeps expectations: human ownership/accountability, ability to explain all changes, licence compliance, authentic (non-"courier") engagement. Explicitly builds on scikit-learn's policy (policy reuse in the wild). Disclosure has since been added and is now threshold-gated: "If generative AI tools were used for substantive portions of the contribution, contributors must describe their use in the change description. This also helps downstream citations comply with academic policies that may require disclosure of genAI usage." Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `disclosure: required` + `exceptions` for the substantiveness threshold. The academic-citation rationale is one no other policy in the corpus gives for disclosure, and it is a *downstream consumer* argument — the disclosure exists to serve someone outside the project.

### Linux Kernel: Good
Accept with strict DCO discipline: "AI agents MUST NOT add Signed-off-by tags. Only humans can legally certify the Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO)"; the human reviews everything and signs off; GPL-2.0/SPDX compliance. Maps strongly: `stance: generated`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `signoff: dco`, `disclosureFormat: {method: commit-trailer, trailer: "Assisted-by:"}`. (GPL-2.0/SPDX licence compliance is a generic contribution rule, not AI-specific.)

**The trailer does not carry a model identity, and the policy has since split in two.** The format is "Assisted-by: LLM [TOOL1] [TOOL2]", example `Assisted-by: LLM coccinelle sparse`, where the optional bracketed items are specialised analysis tools (coccinelle, sparse, smatch, clang-tidy) rather than a model or a version — so the trailer records *that an LLM was involved and what static-analysis tooling it drove*, not which model. `coding-assistants.rst` now covers procedure and the DCO rule and defers content questions to a separate, tool-agnostic `Documentation/process/generated-content.rst`, which governs Coccinelle output, `checkpatch.pl --fix`, translation tools and chatbots under one maintainer-discretion regime rather than a disclosure mandate.

Two things follow for the format. The kernel is the corpus's clearest case of a project deciding the **generating tool's identity is not the useful thing to record** — a deliberate rejection of the model+version disclosure the schema contemplates, not an omission. And by folding AI into one category with mechanical code generators, it declines the premise that AI-generated content needs its own policy surface at all, which is the sharpest available challenge to a format scoped to AI specifically.

### SunPy: Good
Short: if you use generative AI, fully understand the changes and be able to explain why they're correct; a fuller AI-usage doc is linked. The linked doc now carries a mandatory, structured disclosure: "**All pull requests must include the AI Assistance Disclosure checklist**", auto-added to the pull request description, with categories for code generation, test/benchmark generation, documentation, research and understanding, and "No AI tools used". Enforcement is unusually mechanical — pull requests that fail to complete it "will be marked as draft and will not be reviewed", those that remove it "may be immediately closed without a review", and repeated non-compliance "may be considered a code of conduct violation". Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `disclosure: required`, `disclosureFormat.method: pr-template`, `statement`/`guardrailsRef` → the linked doc. A fourth PR-template datapoint alongside Matplotlib, PROJ and QGIS, and the only one that enumerates *what the AI was used for* rather than whether it was used.

### Zulip: Good
Any tools (incl. AI) permitted, but you must understand and explain every change: "the answer to 'why is X an improvement?' should never be 'the AI did it'"; un-understood/untested AI PRs are closed without review. Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review]`. The commit-discipline and "don't ask AI to add comments" guidance is prose / a `comments` assertion.

### attrs: Good (a standalone-file datapoint)
Publishes a **standalone `.github/AI_POLICY.md`**. Doesn't police authoring, but every contribution must be backed by a human who owns the copyright (**no LLM bots in `Co-authored-by:`**), no unsupervised agentic tools, and "DoS-by-slop" earns a permanent ban; DCO-style certification. Maps: `stance: generated`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `humanReview: required`, `signoff: dco`, `enforcement: [denylist]`. The "no LLM in `Co-authored-by`" is an agent-identity/attestation rule (`autonomousAgents.attestation`). That this policy is a *separate file* is direct evidence for the standalone-file approach (standalone `ai-policy.yml`).

### Ladybird: n/a (no policy at all)
Two things make this a non-case. First, it is closed-contribution ("We do not accept pull requests from non-maintainers"), so there is no external-contributor policy to express. Second, **it has no AI policy for its maintainers either**: the `CONTRIBUTING` file carries only a "Human language policy" (tone/style for user-facing strings, comments, and commit messages: American English, no contractions, no non-literal language), but nothing about whether maintainers may use AI to write code. So the honest `ai-policy` state here is simply *absent*, a reminder that "no field" is itself the correct signal for "no declared policy," and that `ai-policy` presupposes an open-contribution model to have a per-scope stance that bites.

### OpenJDK: Good
Interim reject: no LLM/diffusion/deep-learning-generated content in code, text, or images across repos, PRs, email, wiki, and issues; **but private use to comprehend, debug, review, and research is explicitly allowed** so long as nothing generated is contributed. Maps: `summary: disallowed`, `defaults.stance: disallowed` (all scopes), `allowedUses: [research, comprehension, debugging]` and `review.aiAssist: permitted`. A textbook `allowedUses` case, and the "surfaces" (email, wiki, JBS issues) hint that scope could extend beyond the repo.

### Jellyfin: Good
LLM output prohibited for all *direct communication* (issues, feature requests, PR bodies, forum): must be your own words; **LLM-assisted translation exempt if disclosed**. Code: no "pure vibe coding," concise/focused PRs split into discrete commits, must explain in the PR body without LLM text, must test, must handle review feedback yourself. Maps: `stance: generated`, `assertions[{comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions}=disallowed; {translations}=generated+disclose]`, `humanReview: required`, `requirements: [tests-pass, new-tests-required]`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review, denylist]` (ban for code theft). Novel bits → prose/`exceptions`: "don't commit LLM metafiles (`.claude`)", mandatory commit-splitting, and rules for *sharing LLM-built tools* in community spaces.

### NLnet Labs: Good
No LLM output in code or documentation, **but a suggested fix in a vulnerability/bug report may be included** (it aids triage), and LLM linting/analysis/review is permitted; disclosure of LLM use required; machine translation discouraged but allowed if disclosed. Maps: `defaults.stance: disallowed` + `assertions[{scope: security-reports, stance: assisted}]` + `review.aiAssist: permitted` + `disclosure: required`. Note this is a per-scope override that makes `security-reports` **more** permissive: the mirror image of most policies, and a good stress-test that `assertions[]` composes in both directions.

### Castle Game Engine: Good
Accept with heavy responsibility: understand everything you submit (code, docs, commits, issue/PR descriptions), be critical of hallucinations, **keep AI conversations private** (don't clutter issues), prefer generating smaller pieces. Maps: `stance: generated`, `humanReview: required`, `assertions[{comments, issue-descriptions}]` (no pasted AI chat). The "smaller pieces" heuristic and the long personal-voice rationale are naturally `statement` prose.

### Godot: Good (a policy that tightened, and the corpus's clearest triviality threshold)
Godot [replaced its contribution policy on 30 June 2026](https://godotengine.org/article/contribution-policy-2026/), moving from an essentially permissive licence-gated stance to a restrictive one, with review capacity rather than copyright as the stated driver. "No use of AI to generate substantial pieces of code. We require all code to be human authored. AI assistance should be limited to menial things (like code completion, regex, or find and replace)"; "No autonomous AI agent use or vibe coding. This already leads to an auto-ban from our GitHub repository"; "No AI-generated text in human-to-human communication. When our maintainers volunteer their time to review your issue, PR, or proposal, they do not want to talk to a machine", machine translation of human-written text excepted; disclosure in the PR discussion required for any AI use in authoring; no AI-generated user-facing media; and "All PRs must be reviewed and approved by a human before merging."

Maps to `summary: restrictive` with `stance: assisted` — AI may help produce contributed code, but only menially — plus `allowedUses[spelling-grammar, translation]`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false` with `enforcement: [denylist]` (the auto-ban is unusually explicit), `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions]=disallowed`, `assertions[media]=disallowed`, `disclosure: required`, `humanReview`, and `review.humanDecision: required` for "All PRs must be reviewed and approved by a human before merging". The menial uses it permits — code completion, regex, find-and-replace — are why the stance is `assisted` rather than `disallowed`: `allowedUses` covers uses whose output is *not* contributed, and these are assistance with output that is. Reading Godot as `disallowed` would have been a category error, and noticing it is what sharpened the boundary between the two fields.

Two things it contributes that nothing else does. **A stated line count**: the [contribution rules](https://contributing.godotengine.org/en/latest/development/contribution_rules.html) presume code under fifteen lines to be trivial, which is the only place in the corpus where the significance threshold every policy gestures at is written as a number — a second data point after Rust's volume circuit-breaker for the **scope/volume limits stay in prose** choice, and the only one a consumer could act on, because it concerns a single contribution rather than a project-wide rate. The choice was re-examined on the strength of it and kept; see the divergences below. **A boundary marker for what is not an AI rule**: the same rewrite bars new contributors, defined as "someone with 3 or fewer merged pull requests", from features and significant refactoring without maintainer permission. That rule sits *before* the AI section and applies however a contribution was produced, justified as mentorship and trust-building rather than as an AI measure. It is worth recording precisely because it is tempting to score: a policy rewritten under AI pressure can contain rules that are not AI rules, and this one belongs to the general contribution process, not to `ai-policy`.

The licence gate the survey previously scored — AI-generated code is subject to copyright, so only MIT-compatible licences qualify — is no longer the operative rule, having been overtaken by a near-ban on substantial AI authorship.

### GDAL: Good
Rationale as previously captured (the scarce resource is *maintenance*, not code; plus the unresolved US/EU copyright status of LLM output): a strongly **societal/economic** framing that maps to `concerns` and the `models.policy` training-data openness. The operative rules, now extracted, put it close to GCC and AFNix: the human must be the primary author with full understanding, "vibe-coded" contributions are banned, and LLM use "should be limited to improved auto-completion mechanism, or for repeated tasks (mechanical refactoring)"; autonomous-agent submissions are prohibited by name; and disclosure is unusually broad — "*Any* LLM usage must be indicated by ticket label, comment, or commit message indication and account for what was written by whom/what." Accountability is stated at the line level: "The contributing human author is ultimately responsible for every line of code, comment, or mailing list interaction they initiate." Enforcement runs through standardised non-compliance notices requesting justification, escalating to temporary or permanent bans.

Maps: `summary: restrictive` with `stance: assisted`, since the permitted residue (improved autocompletion, mechanical refactoring) is assistance with contributed output rather than an `allowedUses` case, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `disclosure: required` with no significance threshold at all, `enforcement: [maintainer-review, denylist]`. The any-usage disclosure rule is the corpus's strictest, and the only one that explicitly requires apportioning authorship ("what was written by whom/what") rather than merely flagging involvement.

### Flathub: Good (rescored — a store policy that grew a contribution policy)
Previously recorded as out of scope, on the reading that it governed AI-generated *applications and media* submitted to the store rather than contributions. That is no longer what it says. The current Generative AI policy requires submitters to "disclose any AI-generated **code, documentation, packaging**, or other material they know or reasonably believe is included in the application or its Flathub packaging", identifying "the affected parts and approximate extent", with a carve-out that "AI used only for research, discussion, or debugging does not need disclosure when no generated material is included". It bans agents from the submission process outright — "AI tools or agents **must not open or automate Flathub submission pull requests, or generate their commit messages, descriptions, review comments, or replies. Submitters must not request AI-agent reviews**" — and enforces with rejection escalating to "a permanent ban from future submissions and activities", with reviewers keeping discretion to reject on the "extent or role of generated material" regardless of disclosure.

Maps to `disclosure: required` with an extent qualifier, `allowedUses[research, debugging]`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review, denylist]`. The ban on *requesting* an AI-agent review is a rule about what a contributor may ask the project's own review process to do, the same review-domain ground Rust, Fedora and Godot reach from different directions. That ground is now the `review` object: `aiAssist: disallowed` carries Flathub's position, with the request-side wording in `review.notes` until enough projects state it alike to justify an enum.

### Python (CPython devguide): Good
Accept; the submitter is responsible regardless of AI use and must explain changes in their own words; **disclosure is "appreciated" but not required**; acceptable uses = comments (esp. non-native language), understanding code, supplementing knowledge; unproductive/repeat submitters may be blocked. `stance: generated`, `humanReview`, `disclosure: encouraged` (the "appreciated but not required" tier), `allowedUses[research]`, `enforcement:[maintainer-review, denylist]`.

### FastAPI: Good
AI encouraged, but contributions need "meaningful human intervention"; if prompt-effort < review-effort, don't submit ("Human Effort Denial-of-Service"); AI-looking PRs/comments closed; spammers blocked. `stance: generated`, `humanReview`, `assertions[comments]`, `enforcement:[maintainer-review, denylist]`. (Echoes attrs' "DoS-by-slop.")

### AFNix: Good
No AI-generated or AI-derived content, **but non-creative uses tolerated** (summarizing docs, assistive tech with full author control, static-analysis/debugging). Clean `stance: disallowed` + `allowedUses[research, accessibility, debugging]` + `enforcement`.

### postmarketOS: Good
Full ban, and members **may not even recommend AI tools**; CoC-based bans. The fullest societal rationale in the corpus (energy, water, exploited labor, non-consensual training data, FOSS-infra DDoS, layoffs) → `concerns`. Banning *recommendation* is a community-conduct rule → prose.

### Matplotlib: Good
Accept with authentic engagement; acceptable = understanding, solution ideas, translating/proof-reading (keep close to original); unacceptable = external bots/agents interacting, un-understood output, multi-project breadth; a **structured "AI Disclosure" field in the PR template**; AI PRs closed on good-first-issues. `stance: generated`, `disclosureFormat: pr-template`, `allowedUses[research, translation, spelling-grammar]`, `autonomousAgents=false`, and the good-first-issue rule → **`newContributors.issuesHumanOnly`**. The no-multi-project-breadth rule → `exceptions`/prose. Validates the `pr-template` disclosure method.

### pip-tools (Jazzband): Good
Any tools OK, but **don't use LLMs to speak for you in project spaces except translation/grammar** (incl. changelogs, PR descriptions); agents that submit PRs without human review not permitted; don't LLM-solve good-first-issues. `stance: generated`, `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions]=disallowed` + `allowedUses[translation, spelling-grammar]`, `autonomousAgents=false`, and the good-first-issue rule → **`newContributors.issuesHumanOnly`**.

### PROJ: Good (adapted from LLVM)
Human-in-the-loop; label substantial tool-generated content via a **PR-template checkbox + `Assisted-by:` trailer**; bans unapproved agents (`@claude`) and auto-review bots (opt-in human-in-loop OK); "extractive contribution" framing; copyright responsibility. It explicitly says labeling is "to facilitate reviews, **not** to track which parts are generated", direct support for the schema's choice not to model a generated-parts inventory. **A verbatim adaptation of LLVM's policy.**

### KDE (GSoC): Good
Reject: no AI for the proposal, code, MR description, or commit messages; **AI translation discouraged** ("you'll never learn if you don't try"); cites licensing, ethics, and environment. `summary/stance: disallowed`, `concerns`. Like yt-dlp, does **not** exempt translation, reinforcing `allowedUses` is per-project. Program-scoped (GSoC), not necessarily KDE-wide.

### Redox OS: Good
Full ban ("not open to discussion"); clearly-labelled LLM content (issues, MRs, descriptions) is closed; bypass → ban; DCO certification. Even blocks SSH pushes to deter AI scrapers (infra measure → prose). `summary/stance: disallowed`, `humanReview`, `signoff: dco`, `enforcement:[denylist]`.

### curl: Good
Accepts AI-assisted code if it meets standards; **for security reports, AI use MUST be disclosed and independently verified** (fabricated reports → instant ban: the bug-bounty-slop problem); translation welcomed with disclosure only *encouraged*; `Assisted-by:` is among its commit trailers. `stance: generated`, `assertions[security-reports]` (disclosure required), `allowedUses[translation]`, `disclosureFormat: commit-trailer`. Notable: **the disclosure requirement varies by scope** (required for security-reports, encouraged for translation), validating per-scope disclosure in `assertions[]`.

### Oxide (RFD 576): Partial
A values document: LLM use is *encouraged* but always subordinate to human responsibility; per-use guidance (readers/researchers/editors fine, writers discouraged, code-review "not a human substitute," programmers with care). It **bans both LLM *mandates* and LLM *shaming***, the strongest **Contributor opt-out / non-discrimination** evidence in the corpus (bidirectional). Surfaces a new **data-privacy** concern (ensure hosted models don't train on uploaded docs), and is notably *skeptical of blanket disclosure* (it can "distance oneself from responsibility"). Mostly `statement` prose.

### OpenInfra Foundation: Good/Full
One of the best-mapping policies. Predictive/Assistive use → **`Assisted-By:`** label; substantial Generative use → **`Generated-By:`** label: two disclosure tiers keyed by *degree of generation*; recommends **open-source models trained on compatibly-licensed content** (`models.policy`); human-in-loop + `signoff: dco`; enable tool features that flag/block matching public code (`requirements`); an exception for well-documented automated processes (release tooling, i18n → a bot carve-out). The `Generated-By:` label is ASF-derived, more convergence. Its two-tier-by-degree disclosure maps to `disclosureFormat.trailers`.

### W3C Advisory Board: n/a
A **position statement** on LLMs in standards work (opportunities like brainstorming/gap-analysis; risks like subtle incorrectness and "second-hand burdening"; some guardrails): reflective, not an operative contribution policy. `statement`-only.

### Haiku OS: Good (settled via AGENTS.md)
Listed in CHAOSS as a Gerrit review, but it **merged** (July 2026): the Policies section of `AGENTS.md` now reads "**No AI-generated code.** Every contribution must be written by a human. Code, documentation, or any other materials generated or modified with AI assistance are not accepted." A terse, absolute ban reaching every content type, and covering *modification* as well as generation → `summary/stance: disallowed` across scopes. Notable: the policy lives in **`AGENTS.md`**, validating `ai-policy`'s `guardrailsRef` / agent-facing pointer.

### Debian: Good (GR carried; the corpus's widest single deliberation)

The richest stress-test in the corpus: one project weighing the full spectrum at once on a Condorcet ballot, settled 30 Aug 2026. Every drafted option maps onto the schema; the value is in what they collectively surface, and the losing options are as informative as the winner because each is a real, drafted policy text the format has to be able to carry.

**[Choice 5, "Responsible Use of Generative AI", carried](https://www.debian.org/vote/2026/vote_002)** — it was alone in the Schwartz set. Debian neither endorses nor prohibits generative AI: the same quality and licensing standards apply regardless of tooling, contributors are fully responsible for and must understand what they submit, disclosure is encouraged but not required, confidential or sensitive project data must not go to third-party services, and large-scale automated changes need prior discussion. Maps to `summary: mixed`, `disclosure: encouraged`, `humanReview`, `signoff: dco`, `requirements: [no-sensitive-data-to-3p]`, with licence compliance staying in the general contribution process.

| Ballot choice | Stance | Maps via |
|---|---|---|
| 1: No LLM contributions, via the Social Contract | disallowed | `summary/stance: disallowed`, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `concerns` (licensing, quality, labor, environmental); upstream / AI-software excluded → applicability prose |
| 2: Allow AI-assisted contributions with conditions | generated | `disclosure: encouraged` ("strongly recommended"), `disclosureFormat.trailers` (`Generated-By:`/`Assisted-By:`), DFSG tool-terms + third-party-copyright check → `models.policy` + AI-specific `requirements`, `humanReview` + `signoff`; bulk changes need prior discussion (→ `priorArrangement`); `requirements: [no-sensitive-data-to-3p]` |
| 3: Reject LLMs as far as practical, update the CoC | restrictive | **messages to humans must be human-drafted** → `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, …]=disallowed`; `disclosure: required`; maintainer bans → `actorExceptions`; violations = CoC → `enforcement` |
| 4: Accept AI contributions for Debian-specific work | generated | `disclosure: required` in commit/changelog, but tab-completion may go unmarked → `assisted` vs `generated`; DFSG → `models.policy`; personal sign/upload → `signoff`; `no-sensitive-data-to-3p`; Debian-scope-only → applicability |
| **5: Responsible use of generative AI — carried** | assisted/generated | `summary: mixed` + `statement`; `disclosure: encouraged`; `no-sensitive-data-to-3p`; mass actions need discussion; existing standards apply |
| 6: A cautious approach to generative AI | ~restrictive | "prefer human authorship" (soft discouragement) → `summary` + `statement`; `disclosure: encouraged`; maintainer rejection → `actorExceptions` |
| 7: Debian is created by humans | assisted | output may not be contributed *directly*, but assistive use (explore, research, analyze, critique) is fine → `stance: assisted` + `allowedUses`; broad surface (packaging, BTS, Salsa, lists, infrastructure, web, docs, official communications), upstream excluded → applicability prose; rationale is reviewer-burden transfer, the "extractive contribution" concern |
| 8: Avoid LLMs — climate destruction is a deal breaker | *no operative rule* | Explicitly non-binding: a stated position that contributors should avoid LLMs where practical, conceding detection and enforcement are impractical. `concerns: [environmental]` carries it and nothing else does — the purest instance in the corpus of a concern that shapes a decision without becoming a rule |

**Three dimensions it brings out:**

1. **"No sensitive data to third-party / untrusted AI services"**; an operative data-handling rule in Choices 2, 4, 5 and 6 (and Oxide): don't leak embargoed / credential / private project data to hosted models, distinct from the stance. Maps to `requirements: [no-sensitive-data-to-3p]`, and it is now *adopted* rather than proposed.
2. **Autonomous / bulk changes gated on *prior discussion*** (Choices 2 and 5, modeled on Debian's mass-bug-filing): not banned, but process-gated. `autonomousAgents.allowed` is a boolean with no such middle state; the carried option made this a requirement rather than a hypothetical, and `priorArrangement` is the field that answers it.
3. **Environmental concern reached the ballot as its own option and lost.** Choice 8 put climate impact forward as the deciding consideration and was itself drafted as a non-binding preference; the neutral option carried. That is the rationale-versus-rule gap at its sharpest, in the largest deliberation available — see [Specificity](#specificity-against-the-chaoss-domain-taxonomy).

Validations: the disclosure gradient (voluntary → recommended → required) collapses cleanly onto `none | encouraged | required`; degree-keyed trailers (Choice 2); human-only communication → `assertions` (Choice 3); DCO-style personal sign-off (Choice 4) → `signoff`; the AI-specific licence check (DFSG tool-terms, verify third-party copyright) → the output-licence-in-scope reading. The nuance the `stance` enum cannot express is "prefer human authorship" (Choices 3, 6, 8), a *preference* short of a rule, which falls to `summary` + `statement`.

### Rust: Partial (the dimensions that stress-tested the format hardest)

Adopted 6 Aug 2026 for [`rust-lang/rust`](https://forge.rust-lang.org/policies/llm-usage.html), and deliberately narrow: it binds only the teams that ratified it (compiler, libs, types, rustdoc, bootstrap and their subteams), not other repositories, submodules, or the wider project. The organising line is "fine to use LLMs to answer questions, analyze, distill, refine, check, suggest, review — but not to **create**", which is `stance: assisted` as a default with a carved-out, disclosed exception for generated code. Private use is unrestricted; generating a solution privately and then writing it from scratch is explicitly fine. LLM-created code is admitted as a *time-boxed experiment*: non-critical, high-quality, well-tested, well-reviewed changes, with disclosure. Public documentation, doc-comments, safety comments, compiler diagnostics and user-facing text are prohibited outright, as are LLM-written issue bodies and PR descriptions from a personal account; machine translation, trivial changes and bug discovery are conditionally allowed with disclosure. LLM review bots must run under separate, blockable accounts and their comments are advisory only — never sufficient to merge.

Most of that maps cleanly. Three things did not when Rust was first scored, and they are the most valuable findings in the corpus since Debian. The first has since become a field; the other two remain outside the format on purpose:

- **Reviewer pre-agreement.** A reviewer must agree to review the change *before the pull request is opened*; new contributors must discuss it with their assigned reviewer first. This is a consent gate on the *human* contribution path, adjacent to but distinct from gating *agent* actions on prior discussion. The generalised `priorArrangement` field now serves both, with `basis: reviewer-agreement` for this case.
- **A forge label as the disclosure method.** Merged LLM-created PRs carry an `llm-assisted` label. `disclosureFormat.method` knows commit trailers and PR templates; a label is a third, forge-native mechanism, and unlike the other two it is applied by the *reviewer at merge* rather than by the contributor at submission — so it doubles as the project's own measurement instrument.
- **A volume circuit-breaker.** If LLM-created changes exceed half of merged PRs over a six-week window, further LLM-created merges halt until the rate falls back. This is a *proportionality threshold relative to project throughput* — an operative rule keyed to volume rather than to content, which the format deliberately does not express: evaluating it needs merge statistics across a six-week window that no contributor or agent holds, so a declared value could be read but never checked against the thing it governs.

Also notable: enforcement is explicitly **not** detection. Contributors are asked to be honest, deliberate misrepresentation is a Code of Conduct matter, and members are told *not* to investigate whether others used an LLM — the clearest statement in the corpus of the design assumption behind `attestation` (declared identity is the tractable lever, detection is not).

### Codeberg: Partial (a platform policy, not a contribution policy)

The one **forge** in the corpus, and it sits at a different layer from everything else here. Two motions carried by member vote on 22 Jul 2026. The first binds the platform: "The Codeberg forge and its associated services are not and will not use the code or data of projects and users to train 'Artificial Intelligence' tools such as Large Language Models." The second added [Terms of Use §2(1)7](https://codeberg.org/Codeberg/org/src/branch/main/TermsOfUse.md): "You must not share projects that mostly consist of code written by 'generative AI'-tools (including services such as *Claude*, *OpenAI Codex*)", reaching projects created by autonomous agents, projects maintained with heavy LLM use, and projects consuming resources disproportionate to their human users. Established communities with significant pre-LLM history, small low-resource experiments, and individual AI-assisted contributions inside a human-maintained codebase are all exempt. Enforcement is case-by-case moderation with no automated content analysis and no mass deletion.

`ai-policy` reaches the stance and the concerns, and nothing more — because **the operative rules are not addressed to contributors.** One governs what the platform may be used to *host*; the other is a commitment the platform makes about *itself*. `ai-policy` models a project's rules for people contributing to it, so a hosting restriction is recorded rather than scored, and a self-binding data commitment has nowhere to go at all. Flathub is the instructive contrast: it began as the same kind of product-content policy and has since grown contributor-facing rules about code, packaging and agents, which the format does reach — so the layer a policy sits at is a property of the document at a point in time, not a fixed attribute of the organisation publishing it.

That layer mismatch is the finding, not a defect in either document, and it generalises well beyond Codeberg — see [Specificity](#specificity-against-the-chaoss-domain-taxonomy). Codeberg's stated rationale is also the most concrete infrastructure argument on record: SSD prices rising from €700 to €3,700 amid hardware scarcity, with LLM-generated "ghost projects" consuming storage and database capacity out of all proportion to their human users, against donation funding.

### Polars: Good (the prior-arrangement gate, stated plainly)
A standalone `AI_POLICY.md`, following the attrs/Ghostty/Mastodon pattern. Its organising rule is a gate rather than a stance: "Pull requests with AI-generated code can only be for accepted issues" — drive-by AI pull requests referencing no accepted issue are closed on that ground alone. Beyond it: "AI may not be used for issues marked as 'good first issue'", preserving newcomer learning; "AI-generated code must have been fully verified with human use" on every target platform; "All AI usage in any form must be disclosed. You must state the tool(s) you used… along with the extent that the work was AI-assisted"; "Agents are strictly forbidden from interacting with our repository", covering issues, pull requests, comments and reviews alike; and no AI-generated media or non-code text, with code and docstrings the sole exception. Undisclosed but suspected AI use closes the pull request.

Maps: `stance: generated` conditionally, `priorArrangement[ai-generated-prs] basis: accepted-issue`, `humanReview`, `disclosure: required` with tool and extent, `autonomousAgents.allowed: false`, `newContributors.issuesHumanOnly`, `assertions[media]=disallowed`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review]`.

It is the cleanest statement in the corpus of the gate Debian, Rust and Fedora each reach differently — and the most useful, because it is unconditional and mechanical rather than a judgement: an AI pull request either references an accepted issue or it does not. Where Rust's version gates on a reviewer agreeing in advance and Debian's on prior discussion, Polars gates on an artifact that already exists in the tracker, which is the version a machine could check. That is what carried the case for adding `priorArrangement`: the gate can be given a checkable definition rather than only a social one, and Polars is the entry that demonstrates it.

### Fedora: Good (and the only policy in the corpus that governs AI in governance decisions)

Adopted by the Fedora Council on 22 October 2025 as the [Policy on AI-Assisted Contributions](https://communityblog.fedoraproject.org/council-policy-proposal-policy-on-ai-assisted-contributions/), and the most-exercised policy here by elapsed time — nearly a year of practice, which its author now considers grounds for revision. The contribution half maps cleanly: AI is framed as "a tool to empower our contributors" with human oversight critical; "the contributor is always the author and is fully accountable for their contributions", who must review, test and understand what they submit; disclosure goes in "your pull request description, commit message, or wherever authorship is normally indicated", with `Assisted-by: <name of code assistant>` as the suggested trailer — another independent convergence on that trailer; submitting "unverified or low-quality machine-generated content" is prohibited on the reviewer-burden rationale that recurs across the corpus; minor assistance such as grammar checking needs no disclosure.

Two rules reach past what the format models. Reviewers may use AI to assist feedback but may not "wholly automate the review process" or let it make the final acceptance determination — the same advisory-only shape Rust surfaced, and together they are what earned `review.humanDecision: required`, where the old `allowedUses: code-review-assist` could record only a bare permission. And AI/ML tools may not be used to score submissions for **code of conduct matters, funding requests, conference talks, or leadership positions** — a rule about AI in the project's own *decision-making*, addressed to whoever runs those processes rather than to contributors. `ai-policy` deliberately does not reach it, and no other policy surveyed has an operative rule there at all: it is the [Moderation domain](#specificity-against-the-chaoss-domain-taxonomy) the CHAOSS taxonomy scores as universally absent, and it turns out to be absent because the corpus was not looking at policies written by a project that also runs a foundation's governance.

Large-scale AI initiatives are excluded from the policy and handled case by case with the Council — a third instance of the prior-arrangement gate, alongside Debian's and Rust's.

Fedora is also the corpus's only project pairing a prose policy with a published, machine-readable set of *sanctioned* workflows: its AI/ML SIG maintains a [skills library](https://forge.fedoraproject.org/ai-ml/skills-library) in the [Agent Skills](https://agentskills.io/specification) format, one `SKILL.md` per workflow, covering compose triage, FTBFS bug search, meeting-log summarisation and Packit onboarding. That is the shape `guardrailsRef` anticipates, arrived at independently and in a standard format — and it points at a distinction the field does not draw, since a guard-rail checklist constrains what an agent may do while a skills library declares what the project has already blessed.

## Cross-cutting findings

Across every settled policy analyzed, the schema captures the **tier and major boundaries** at the signal level. Two policies carry an operative rule the format still does not reach, both recorded below: Rust's volume circuit-breaker and Fedora's bar on AI scoring governance decisions, the second of which is [out of scope by design](./README.md#what-this-format-does-not-govern) rather than missing. What recurs:

**Vindicated by real policies:**

- **Disclosure trailers converge** on exactly the `disclosureFormat` shape: `Assisted-by:` (Mastodon, LLVM, GCC, Linux Kernel, curl, PROJ, QGIS, Fedora, OpenInfra) and `Generated-by:` (ASF, OpenInfra). What they do *not* converge on is **what the disclosure must name**. The Linux Kernel's trailer is `Assisted-by: LLM [TOOL…]`, where the optional items are static-analysis tools rather than a model or version, and Gitea dropped its model-naming clause; KeePassXC requires the service or model, and Polars requires "the tool(s) you used… along with the extent that the work was AI-assisted". `disclosureFormat` currently describes *where* a disclosure goes (method, trailer strings) and says nothing about *what it must contain*, which happens to be the right call given the divergence — but it means a consumer cannot tell a bare `Assisted-by:` regime from one requiring tool identity and extent. Whether that content requirement deserves a field is an open question, not a settled omission.
- **`allowedUses` earns its place**: QEMU, Servo, GCC, Forgejo all carve out research / debugging / comprehension / translation / spelling as explicitly permitted; without it these read as pure bans.
- **`allowedUses` is genuinely per-project, not a fixed exempt-set**: most exempt AI *translation* (Forgejo, Mastodon, GCC), but **yt-dlp bans it**; GCC *permits* AI code-review while **Forgejo and Mastodon forbid it**. Confirms the "a project lists the uses it permits" design and the note that omission ≠ exempt.
- **Size/significance thresholds recur** (Mastodon "significant portion", KeePassXC "vast majority", GCC "legally significant", Fedora "significant part… without changes", Astropy "substantive portions", Flathub "approximate extent", Polars "extent"); almost all are handled by `exceptions: true` + prose rather than a dedicated threshold field, because each project draws the line its own way and hardly any draw it mechanically. Godot is the exception that was tested and did not change the choice; see the divergences below.
- **`enforcement: denylist` is heavily exercised**: public "denounce" lists and bans dominate (Ghostty, Mastodon, yt-dlp, Teamtype, attrs, scikit-learn).
- **`exceptions: true` is the workhorse**; nearly every "Good" (vs "Full") rating rests on it: it absorbs legal tests (ASF), size thresholds, narrow-vs-general-AI splits (Forgejo), and behavioural triggers (scikit-learn) without bloating the schema.
- **Per-scope overrides go both directions**: most policies make a scope *stricter* (`media`, `security-reports`), but GCC (`tests`) and NLnet (`security-reports`) make one *more permissive*. `assertions[]`'s symmetric override handles both, and `security-reports` shows up on both sides, evidence the scope vocabulary is pulling real weight.
- **"Communication must be your own words, translation/grammar exempted" is the single most common per-scope rule** (Forgejo, Jellyfin, Mastodon, pip-tools, Matplotlib, Gitea, curl) → `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions]=disallowed` + `allowedUses[translation, spelling-grammar]`. This one pattern exercises both the scope overrides and the allowed-uses list at once.
- **PR-template checkboxes are a dominant disclosure method** (Matplotlib, PROJ, QGIS, SunPy) alongside commit trailers → validates `disclosureFormat.method: pr-template`.
- **Policies converge and get copied verbatim, and the lineages are now documented in the policies themselves**: PROJ and QGIS adapt LLVM; Astropy adapts scikit-learn; OpenInfra adopts ASF's `Generated-by:`; Mastodon credits CloudNativePG, Ghostty and the Linux Foundation by name; yt-dlp credits the Zig project's policy and stb's `.NO_AI` convention. Projects are not merely converging — they are citing their sources, which is what a reusable declaration would formalise, and is independent support for the standalone-file approach.

**Candidate additions surfaced (feed the Design Questions):**

- **"AI code review" is a distinct, recurring activity, and it is now its own field**: permitted by some (GCC, OpenJDK, NLnet Labs, PROJ), banned by others (Forgejo, Mastodon, Flathub), permitted-but-never-deciding by others again (Rust, Fedora, Godot). It was briefly treated as a candidate `scope` value; it is not one, because review is done *to* a contribution rather than being one. `review.aiAssist` and `review.humanDecision` carry it, and `allowedUses: code-review-assist` was retired.
- **Named tool allow/deny lists**: QEMU bans specific products by name; ASF and the Linux Foundation *deliberately refuse* to keep approved lists. The schema has only openness-based `models.policy`, no tool-name list; an open candidate, though the ASF/LF refusal suggests *not* standardizing it may be correct.
- **Output-licence: only the AI-specific part is in scope**: generic licence compatibility (a contribution must match the project's licence) applies to *all* contributions and stays in the general contribution process, out of `ai-policy`. What is AI-specific is the risk that a model reproduces incompatibly-licensed *training data*, or that a tool's terms restrict its output; captured by `models.policy` (require permissive/disclosed training data) and by checks some policies mandate: ASF and OpenInfra ask contributors to enable tool features that flag or block output matching public code (a candidate `requirements` gate). Godot's "AI-generated code may carry copyright: check the licence" was that AI-specific check, and is instructive for having been superseded: when Godot rewrote its policy in June 2026 the licence gate gave way to a near-ban justified by review capacity, so the one policy in the corpus built on the copyright theory abandoned it for a workload theory. The check itself remains the right shape; the corpus no longer has a project resting on it.
- **Contributor autonomy is real and bidirectional**: Oxide bans both AI *mandates* and AI *shaming*; SFC bans coercion. The **Contributor opt-out / non-discrimination** question weighs a `contributorAutonomy` declaration.
- **Prior arrangement is a recurring gate, and it is not only about agents**: Debian's carried option treats bulk/automated changes as *allowed-with-coordination* rather than banned (modeled on mass-bug-filing), and Rust requires a reviewer to agree *before* an LLM-created pull request is opened. Fedora excludes large-scale AI initiatives from its policy entirely and sends them to the Council case by case. `autonomousAgents.allowed` is a boolean with no middle state, and nothing covered the human path at all. This is the one candidate in this list that has since been acted on: `priorArrangement` was added to the schema, covering both paths, with `appliesTo` naming the trigger and `basis` naming what satisfies it.
- **A forge label as a disclosure method**: Rust marks merged LLM-created pull requests with an `llm-assisted` label. `disclosureFormat.method` knows commit trailers and PR templates, both applied by the contributor at submission; a label is applied by the *reviewer at merge*, which makes it the project's own measurement instrument as much as a disclosure. A third method value, and the first one that is forge-native.
- **Countable thresholds exist, and mostly cannot be consumed**: Rust halts further LLM-created merges above half of merged pull requests over a six-week window, and Godot presumes code under fifteen lines trivial. Every other threshold in the corpus is a significance judgement left to prose. Examined together these produced a test rather than a field: a threshold earns one only if a consumer can evaluate it before submitting. The prose choice stands, and the test is the reusable part; the reasoning is in the divergences below.
- **Advisory-only review, and bot identity**: Rust permits LLM review bots only under separate, user-blockable accounts with non-blocking comments, and forbids treating LLM review as sufficient to merge; Fedora independently draws the same line, permitting AI to assist a reviewer but never to be the sole or final arbiter. `allowedUses: code-review-assist` was a permission and expressed none of this, which is why review became its own object: `aiAssist` for whether, `humanDecision` for whether it suffices, and `notes` for the bot-account and comment-blocking texture that only one project states so far.
- **Sanctioned workflows are appearing as artifacts, not prose**: Fedora publishes a [skills library](https://forge.fedoraproject.org/ai-ml/skills-library) in the [Agent Skills](https://agentskills.io/specification) format alongside its policy. A declaration that can point at one gives an agent something executable rather than a restatement of the rules, but the direction is opposite to `guardrailsRef`'s — blessing rather than constraining — so whether it is the same pointer is a design question, not an oversight.
- **AI in the project's own decisions is a surface the format does not have**: Fedora forbids using AI/ML tools to score submissions for code-of-conduct matters, funding requests, conference talks and leadership positions. This is not a contribution rule — it binds whoever runs those processes, the way Codeberg's training commitment binds the forge — and it is the first operative instance in either survey of the governance-and-moderation ground the CHAOSS taxonomy scores as empty. It belongs with the [declaring-party axis](#specificity-against-the-chaoss-domain-taxonomy) rather than with `scope`. Governance-process rules are [out of scope by design](./README.md#what-this-format-does-not-govern), carried as `notes` plus `exceptions: true` by any project that holds one. Fedora remains the strongest evidence that the axis is real, since one organisation publishes a contributor rule and a governance rule in a single document and only one of them is a contribution rule at all.

**Observations that validate current choices:**

- **Disclosure is *not* universal, but the exceptions are thinning.** Zulip still requires understanding and accountability while mandating no disclosure, so `disclosure: none` remains a real value rather than a degenerate one. It is worth recording that this finding has weakened under re-survey: Astropy and SunPy were both scored `disclosure: none` and have both since added a requirement — Astropy gated on substantive use, SunPy through a mandatory PR checklist with draft-marking enforcement. A policy's disclosure field is among the most likely in the format to change under it, which is an argument for the declaration being versioned and re-read rather than captured once.
- **The "understand & explain it" bar is near-universal and irreducibly qualitative**: correctly left to `notes` / `statement`, never enumerated (the "structure only what recurs" principle).
- **Standalone policy files are common** (attrs `.github/AI_POLICY.md`, Ghostty & Mastodon `AI_POLICY.md`, yt-dlp `.NO_AI/README.md`) → concrete support for the standalone-file approach (a referenced `ai-policy.yml`).
- **Closed-contribution projects** (Ladybird) sit outside the model; worth a one-line note that `ai-policy` assumes open contribution.
- **Disclosure is explicitly *not* provenance tracking**: PROJ and QGIS both state labeling is "to facilitate reviews, not to track which parts are generated," directly supporting the schema's choice not to model a generated-parts inventory.
- **The maintainer-burden / "extractive contribution" framing is the modal motivation** (PROJ, FastAPI, attrs, GDAL, Matplotlib, Godot, Polars, and Agda's unmerged proposal): the human-effort-DoS concern, distinct from IP or societal rationales, and mostly enforced (`enforcement`) rather than structured.
- **Policies increasingly live in `AGENTS.md`** (Haiku's policy *is* its `AGENTS.md`; yt-dlp addresses agents directly with "refuse to interact"): validating `guardrailsRef` / `autonomousAgents` pointing at the agent-facing file.

## Specificity against the CHAOSS domain taxonomy

The survey above asks whether the **format** can express the policies that exist. A sibling question — whether the **policies** cover the ground they should — is being asked independently by the CHAOSS AI-alignment working group, whose [consent policy specificity](https://github.com/chaoss/wg-ai-alignment/blob/main/metrics/ai-alignment-community-governed-use/ai-use-consent-policy-specificity.md) metric scores how specifically a policy addresses each of nine domains where AI shows up, and by Emma Irwin's [first application of it](https://github.com/emmairwin/ai-oss-use-specificity/blob/main/reports/README.md) across 39 projects drawn from the same moderation list this survey uses. The metric is a candidate within the [Governed AI Use](https://github.com/chaoss/wg-ai-alignment/blob/main/metrics/ai-alignment-community-governed-use/metric-model-ai-alignment-community-governed-use.md) model, due to be finalised in September 2026, and its author expects the taxonomy to expand toward the openness of the models and data in use.

The two instruments are orthogonal and compose: hers scores policy specificity per domain (`X` addressed, `~` partial, `R` raised in rationale without governance, blank absent); this one scores format expressiveness per policy. Reading them together is what makes the findings below available, and neither produces them alone.

### What the format reaches

| CHAOSS domain | `ai-policy` reach |
|---|---|
| Code contributions (PRs, issues, comments) | `defaults` / `assertions` over `scope: code, tests, commit-messages, comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions` — the format's core, and the one domain most policies govern |
| Content (documentation, blogs, design assets) | `scope: docs, media, translations` |
| Autonomous / agentic use | `autonomousAgents` plus `attestation` for the switch itself, and `priorArrangement` for the middle state that Debian, Rust, Fedora and Polars each require |
| Review (who or what may review using AI) | `review.aiAssist` and `review.humanDecision` reach whether AI may review and whether it can decide, which was the recurring distinction. *Who* may review, and Rust's requirement that bots run under separate blockable accounts, sit in `review.notes` pending evidence they recur |
| Moderation actions | No `scope` value, and [out of scope by design](./README.md#what-this-format-does-not-govern). Most policies only near-miss it, reaching moderation by referring violations to a code of conduct, which governs the *response* to bad contributions rather than AI's use in moderating. Fedora is the one operative exception, barring AI from scoring code-of-conduct matters |
| Notetaker / meeting bots | No `scope` value, and no policy in either survey governs it — while Fedora's [skills library](https://forge.fedoraproject.org/ai-ml/skills-library) ships a `meeting-secretary` skill that turns Meetbot logs into published summaries, so the domain is unpoliced rather than unused |
| Environmental impact | `concerns: environmental` — motivation, deliberately not a rule surface |
| Infrastructure strain | Nothing, in `concerns` or elsewhere |
| Data use for training (platform user data) | `models.policy.trainingData` covers the training data of models a project *consumes*; nothing covers a platform binding itself over data it *holds* |

### The `concerns` field is the format's answer to the rationale-versus-rule gap

The headline finding of the specificity work is a gap between what motivates a policy and what it governs: environmental impact shaped the reasoning of several projects and became a rule in none, and infrastructure strain did the same. Establishing that took a second manual pass over preambles and blog posts after the policy texts themselves had been scored, and it earned a distinct code (`R`) in the results.

That distinction is already structural here. [`concerns`](./ai-policy.schema.yaml) is defined as a *motivation signal* — `code-quality`, `security`, `ip-provenance`, `licensing-ethics`, `data-consent`, `labor-ethics`, `skill-preservation`, `environmental`, `oss-sustainability` — carried separately from `defaults`, `assertions` and `requirements`, which carry rules. A project that publishes `ai-policy.yml` states in one machine-readable place both what it governs and what moved it, and the gap between them is then a query rather than a research project. Two entries in this survey make the point on their own: Debian put climate impact on the ballot as its own option, drafted from the outset as a non-binding preference, and the neutral option carried; Servo and postmarketOS both rest bans on societal rationale that only `concerns` carries.

This is the strongest external validation the format has received for a design choice made on other grounds, and it is the most useful thing this survey can offer the working group in return: the metric's stated implementation is manual audit plus text analysis of prose, and the `R`/rule distinction it has to reconstruct by hand is a field.

### Three domains are forge-level, not project-level

Notetaker bots, infrastructure strain and data use for training score zero across 39 projects, and environmental impact scores zero as a rule. The natural reading is that projects have not got to them yet. Fedora rules that reading out for at least one of them: its AI/ML SIG publishes a `meeting-secretary` skill that converts Meetbot logs into Discussion summary posts and ticket comments, so the project both performs the activity and distributes the tooling for it, while its adopted contribution policy says nothing about it. The silence is not inattention to the practice. The Codeberg entry above suggests a different one: for three of them a project policy is the wrong instrument, because the thing to be governed is not the project's to govern.

Codeberg is the one forge in the corpus, and it is where those domains suddenly have rules. It bound itself never to train on hosted code or user data, and it restricted what may be hosted on explicitly infrastructure-and-environment grounds. In the specificity scoring all of that lands as `R` — raised in rationale, not governed — which is correct against an instrument that scores rules addressed to contributors, and which is exactly why the finding is interesting: the strongest instance of data-use-for-training governance in the corpus reads as rationale because it is a rule the platform binds *itself* to, and the taxonomy has no slot for that. Meeting bots belong to whoever runs the meeting infrastructure; server load belongs to whoever pays for the servers; training on hosted code belongs to whoever holds it.

Fedora is independent support from both directions. Its broader draft policy structures itself around contributions, users, *Fedora-as-platform*, and *Fedora-data-for-AI* — a project that hosts infrastructure recognising the layer split unprompted, and separating the platform-facing areas from the contribution-facing ones. Its *adopted* contribution policy then does the same thing in miniature by carrying a governance rule (no AI scoring of code-of-conduct matters, funding requests, talks or leadership) inside a document otherwise addressed to contributors — the declaring party changing mid-document, with nothing marking the change.

So the finding to feed back is not that projects are silent on these domains but that the **domains have the wrong subject**: they need a declaring party axis (project / forge / foundation) before an absence can be read as a gap. This is also where a declaration format has something to offer that a survey cannot — a forge-level record, discoverable at `.well-known`, is a natural carrier for commitments of exactly this shape, and none exists today. `ai-policy` responded by drawing the line rather than stretching to cover it: the format now states [what it does not govern](./README.md#what-this-format-does-not-govern) and why, so that a rule landing outside it reads as belonging to a missing document rather than as an unfilled field. Specifying that document is a live question for this project.

### Divergences kept on purpose

The metric scores four consent attributes per domain. Two map, two are deliberately unstructured here:

- **Supervision level** (banned → fully unsupervised) is one axis where the format keeps two: `stance` for what may be contributed, `autonomousAgents` for what may act unattended. The corpus supports the split — the Linux Kernel accepts generated code while barring agents from signing off — and collapsing them would lose that.
- **Accountability holder** maps partially: `accountability: contributor` and `signoff` establish *that* a human is answerable, never *which* role. No policy surveyed names a role rather than "the contributor", so the gap is untested rather than known-harmful.
- **Scope and volume limits** stay in prose by design: projects draw significance thresholds many different ways and almost none mechanically, so `exceptions: true` plus `statement` carries them. Two counter-examples have since appeared, Rust's volume circuit-breaker and Godot's fifteen-line presumption, and the choice was revisited on them and kept. The reason is that they differ in kind: Godot's concerns one contribution and can be evaluated by whoever holds the diff, while Rust's needs project-wide merge history and answers a different question, whether the door is currently open. Only the consumable sort would justify a field, and on that count the evidence is a single project.
- **Proportionality relative to community size** likewise stays in prose, and is the attribute the format is least likely to adopt: it is a judgement a maintainer makes, not a value a file declares.

### Corpus overlap

The two surveys overlap heavily and neither contains the other. **Polars** came from the specificity corpus and is now scored above; **QGIS** is scored in both, and the specificity corpus scores a second GDAL surface. **Codeberg** and **Rust** came from it and are now scored above — both earned their place, Codeberg for the layer mismatch and Rust for dimensions nothing else in the corpus surfaced. Going the other way, this survey covers curl, FastAPI, OpenJDK, CPython, Redox OS, Haiku OS, Flathub, Ladybird and the W3C Advisory Board, which the specificity corpus does not.

## Under discussion / proposed (not settled)

These appear in the CHAOSS list as open discussions or proposals rather than adopted policies, scored tentatively against whatever direction the thread indicates, and kept separate from the settled policies above.

- **QGIS**: [QEP-408](https://github.com/qgis/QGIS-Enhancement-Proposals/blob/master/qep-408-ai-tool-policy.md), now merged into the QEP repository. Human-in-the-loop; review all LLM output before requesting review; label substantial tool-generated content (`Assisted-by:` trailer); "labeling facilitates review, not provenance tracking." **A near-verbatim adaptation of PROJ/LLVM** (same author). Maps as PROJ does. It stays in this section for a reason worth recording: the document is adopted but still states that "It is not currently a settled question within the QGIS community whether code created by LLMs is acceptable in the QGIS codebase at all", so it governs *how* AI contributions are handled without settling *whether* they are accepted. `ai-policy` does not make QGIS invent the answer: `undecided: [defaults.stance]` with `status: under-discussion` publishes what QEP-408 settles, the human-in-the-loop and labelling rules, without claiming the answer it withholds. Because QEP-408 regulates how AI contributions are handled in the meantime, the stance can keep `generated` as its interim value while staying listed as open. QGIS is the case that shaped `undecided`.
- **Agda**: proposed CONTRIBUTING.md, [PR #8456](https://github.com/agda/agda/pull/8456) — **closed without merging** (April 2026). Blanket ban on LLM-generated contributions incl. issues and issue-tracker discussion; copyright (US non-copyrightability), societal (EFF), and deskilling rationale. Tentative: reject, **Good** (`summary/stance: disallowed` across code + issues, `enforcement: [denylist]`, `concerns`) — but the mapping is now of a text that was not adopted. Whether Agda landed an equivalent policy by another route has not been checked; a closed pull request establishes only that this one did not carry.
- **Kubernetes: disclosure trailer — not adopted**, [community #8558](https://github.com/kubernetes/community/issues/8558), now closed. It proposed standardizing a **`Co-developed-by:`** trailer to indicate tools used; the thread ran into selection-bias and enforceability objections, was redirected to steering, and closed without a convention. So the corpus has two trailer families, not three — `Assisted-by:` and `Generated-by:` — and the one serious attempt at a third failed on enforceability, which is an argument for `disclosureFormat` describing what projects do rather than prescribing a vocabulary.
- **Kubernetes: a position was reached, and it is deliberate non-policy**, [steering #291](https://github.com/kubernetes/steering/issues/291), now closed. The steering committee settled on giving no guidance targeting generative AI specifically, deferring to SIGs where they want it, and leaning on existing contribution-quality guidance instead. This is not "no position yet": it is a considered decision that AI needs no dedicated rule, the same conclusion the Linux Kernel reached by folding AI into tool-generated content generally. For `ai-policy` it was the case the format handled worst — the absence of a file is defined as *no declared policy*, which could not be distinguished from a project that has decided one is unnecessary. `summary: neutral` now closes that gap: it declares that no AI-specific rule applies and points `statement` at the ordinary contribution rules, and [`examples/neutral.yml`](./examples/neutral.yml) is Kubernetes' position as a file.
- **SciPy**, [discussion](https://discuss.scientific-python.org/t/a-policy-on-generative-ai-assisted-contributions/1702). Leaning "blocking wholesale is futile; treat AI assistants like any other tool, with copyright/licence boundaries." Tentative: accept-with-conditions; would map to `stance: assisted/generated` + `humanReview`. Not codified.
- **Fedora (the broader AI policy)**: DRAFT, [thread](https://discussion.fedoraproject.org/t/ai-policy-in-fedora-wip/144297). Distinct from the adopted contribution policy scored above, and wider: structured around four areas (contributions, users, Fedora-as-platform, Fedora-data-for-AI); "prefer your own voice over a bland averaged LLM." Most of it sits **outside** `ai-policy` by design — the three non-contribution areas are platform-level. Still a draft.
- **Ansible**, [thread](https://forum.ansible.com/t/ansible-ai-policy/42642). Open "should we have a policy?" citing Servo's ban as a model. No position yet → n/a.

Nothing is now carried as partially captured: Godot, GDAL and Polars have all been fetched and scored above.

## Template policies

[ai-policy.dev](https://ai-policy.dev/) publishes ready-made `AI_POLICY.md` texts for projects to copy, rather than a policy of its own, so its presets are scored here, and expressed as files in [`examples/`](./examples/), rather than in the Summary table: an adoption of one shows up in that table under the adopting project's name. Read on 25 September 2026 and updated on 27 September against [pull request #25](https://github.com/43081j/ai-policy/pull/25), approved but not yet merged, which adds *AI Disclosed* and *Human Copyright* and tightens *AI Disallowed* and *Human Responsible*; the rows and files follow that revision. The pull request says its additions came from having an AI read about 600 policies found on GitHub, but publishes neither the list nor the method, so it cannot be checked against this survey. Each preset closes on the project's ordinary contribution guidelines, which are not AI rules and are not scored.

| Preset | Tier | Fit | Maps via | Needs prose / gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI Allowed | accept | Full | summary=permissive, stance=generated, `disclosure: none` ("AI usage does not need to be declared"), `autonomousAgents.allowed=true` | licence compatibility → not AI-specific, prose |
| Human Voice | accept-with-conditions | Good | stance=generated, `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions]=disallowed`, `humanReview: required`, `concerns.values: [code-quality]` with "real connection" in `concerns.notes` | "Never let an LLM think for you", and its bar on quoting a model in issues → `notes`/prose; silent on agents |
| Human Responsible | restrict | Good | summary=restrictive, stance=assisted, `assertions[comments, issue-descriptions, pull-request-descriptions, commit-messages]=disallowed`, **`disclosure: prohibited`**, `humanReview: required`, **`requirements: [reproduced, provenance-attested]`**, `autonomousAgents.allowed=false`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review, denylist]`, `exceptions`, "built by people" in `concerns.notes` | the promise that a contributor's tooling "will not be asked about" → prose; closure on suspicion alone → prose |
| AI Disclosed | accept-with-conditions | Good | stance=generated, **`disclosure: required`**, `disclosureFormat.method: free-text` with tools and extent in `notes`, `humanReview: required`, `assertions[media]=disallowed`, `autonomousAgents.allowed=false`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review]`, its rationale (disclosure helps reviewers calibrate) in `concerns.notes` | what the disclosure must name → `notes` (the open *Disclosure content* question); autocompletion and spell-checking exempt → `notes` |
| Human Copyright | accept-with-conditions | Good | stance=generated, `humanReview: required`, `requirements: [provenance-attested]`, `concerns.values: [ip-provenance]` with the licence risk in `concerns.notes`, `autonomousAgents.allowed=false`, `enforcement: [maintainer-review, denylist]` | **no AI in `Co-authored-by:` or any author credit** → `notes`; attrs holds the same rule. Read as `stance: generated` because its authorship requirement is a legal claim ("or have the legal right to submit it"), not a limit on tooling, unlike *Human Responsible*'s |
| AI Disallowed | reject | Full | summary=disallowed, stance=disallowed, **`allowedUses: []`** (not even translation, spelling or bug-finding) | the burden of proof sits with the contributor ("rejected unless the contributor can prove otherwise") → prose |

*Human Responsible* supplied two values the format lacked. It forbids disclosure outright ("Do not add notes about AI tools to your submissions"), the opposite pole from `disclosure: required`, and one a consumer must act on: an agent that appends an `Assisted-by:` trailer by habit breaks this policy with every commit, and a `none` value that means only *not asked for* cannot warn it off. Oxide's scepticism of blanket disclosure points the same way without going as far. It also refuses "Reports or fixes for problems you have not reproduced yourself", a gate a submitter can check before submitting and so one that passes the test the format applies to any countable rule; curl's demand that AI-assisted security reports be independently verified answers the same concern in narrower form. Both are now schema values: `disclosure: prohibited` and the `reproduced` requirement.

*AI Disallowed*'s revision exercises a distinction the format already draws but no example used: an explicitly empty `allowedUses` says no use is exempt, where an absent one leaves the question to the stance. It is the deliberate opposite of the research-only pattern below.

The site's own rule vocabulary is a list of neutral rule names that each preset sorts into permits, requires and forbids, without scopes, and it stays on the website: the frontmatter carrying it is stripped when a policy is copied. That makes it a useful cross-check on this format's field set rather than a competing format. Every one of its rules maps onto a field here except `guidelines`, which is not AI-specific; `private-use`, which is the non-inquiry promise above; and `ai-coauthor`, the ban on crediting an AI as an author. That last one now appears in both *Human Copyright* and attrs, each naming `Co-authored-by:`, which is how a `notes` sentence becomes a candidate for a field. Whether a declaration should name the template its prose came from is the open *Template provenance* question in the [README](./README.md#open-questions-named).

### How much of the corpus the presets cover

Grouping the settled policies in the Summary table by their operative fields gives a handful of recurring patterns. The grouping is approximate, since several policies straddle two, but it shows where presets help:

| Pattern | Examples | Preset |
|---|---|---|
| Generated code accepted, disclosure expected, no agents | LLVM, PROJ, Fedora, curl, Astropy, SunPy, CPython, Matplotlib, Linux Kernel, OpenInfra, Rust, Polars | *AI Disclosed* |
| No generated contributions, research and debugging allowed | OpenJDK, QEMU, Servo, Forgejo, NLnet Labs, AFNix, GCC | none; [`research-only.yml`](./examples/research-only.yml) |
| Outright ban | Gentoo, Redox OS, Haiku OS, postmarketOS, KDE, yt-dlp | *AI Disallowed* |
| Menial assistance only | Godot, GDAL, Ghostty, Mastodon | *Human Responsible*, roughly |
| Accepted, own voice, anti-slop | Zulip, FastAPI, Jellyfin, pip-tools, Castle Game Engine | *Human Voice* |
| Human ownership and licensing | attrs | *Human Copyright* |
| Open | Oxide | *AI Allowed* |

The largest pattern is the one *AI Disclosed* now covers, and it had no preset before pull request #25. The research-only pattern is the clearest remaining gap: it is common, distinct in intent from an outright ban (its projects keep AI for understanding code while refusing it for producing contributions), and *AI Disallowed*'s revision moved away from it rather than towards it. [`research-only.yml`](./examples/research-only.yml) expresses it, modelled on OpenJDK, as the structured half of a preset proposed upstream. The policies that gate contributions on prior agreement (Rust, Polars, Fedora, Debian) fit *AI Disclosed* plus a `priorArrangement`, an addition rather than a preset of its own.

### Early adopters

Searched on 25 September 2026, in the week the site launched, for repositories whose `AI_POLICY.md` links to ai-policy.dev. Every file found is a verbatim copy of its preset.

This is a lower bound: it covers GitHub only, code search indexes recent changes late, and a copy whose closing link was removed is found only by its wording, which turned up nothing further. The npmx.dev, agentscan and agentscan-action adoptions were all made by a contributor to ai-policy.dev itself, so TanStack Query and fast-check are the independent signal.

For this format the pattern says several things. Nobody has chosen either end of the range: every adopter wants a human answering for what is submitted, which is the `humanReview` and per-scope territory where the structure does most of its work. No adopter has edited its preset, so a preset name currently identifies a policy exactly, which is what *Template provenance* would rely on and which only versioned presets would keep true as copies age. And none publishes anything machine-readable, since the site strips its own metadata on copy — the gap the [`examples/`](./examples/) files are meant to fill. agentscan, whose repository describes it as "Automation pattern detection for open source maintainers", is also a candidate *consumer*: a tool that flags agent-like accounts is the natural reader of `autonomousAgents.allowed`.
